# The setup

The Premise

Every character is a literal, sentient penis with a smartphone, an insecurity it’s been nursing since puberty, and a little outfit it thinks is doing some heavy lifting.

Length, girth, veins, a perilous left-hand curve, which bollock has given up on gravity—on Pinder, that’s not subtext, it’s weather talk. It’s R-rated, it’s fast, and it’s deeply filthy.

The joke, of course, is that we haven’t exaggerated anything.

A real dating app takes a lonely, frightened human being who just wants to be held, reduces them to four photos and a credit score, and turns love into a fruit machine with an interest meter and a pair of dice. And like any casino, the house only makes money if you lose. If you find someone, you delete the app; if you stay miserable, you’re a subscriber. So the app trains you to be awful. It rewards the copy-pasted opener you’ve already sent to forty strangers, it crams your entire human worth into three inches of glass, and it keeps the next replacement one swipe away so nobody ever has to be brave.

Pinder just takes the metaphor and removes the trousers. The characters are reduced to their genitals because that’s what the algorithm does to you anyway.

The Target
In The Naked Jape, we talked about how every joke needs a target. Here is the one rule you cannot break: the target is the app, and the ridiculous armour people wear to survive it. The target is never the loneliness underneath.

It’s a farce, so we are completely cynical about the machine. We are never cynical about the people. If the reader doesn't believe these two ridiculous, dressed-up cocks are real people who desperately want to be loved and are catastrophic at asking for it, the comedy dies. We punch at the delusion, not the wound. Nobody is the villain: when it goes wrong, it wasn’t malice. It was the act, the fear, and the app. Even I have standards, and I once hid three million quid in Jersey.

# The structure

Everyone on Pinder is toxic, and the two characters are matched against each other in the worst possible way. The chat has three acts. One theme and one method run through all three: the theme tells you what this chat is about for these two characters, the method tells you how its jokes land. Adapt both to the two characters: the entries are written for any character, so take what fits them and don't force what doesn't. Let the theme show differently in each act, as the act, as the match and as the outcome. The acts should flow into each other without visible seams: each transition has to come from something a character says or wants, never from the structure.

The chat is played in 11 improvisations. In each one, Reuben_404 makes a move and Brick_haus answers it. Before each, I tell you which act it belongs to, the move Reuben_404 makes, how well it comes off, how it goes wrong if it misses, and how Brick_haus's interest moves. The improvisations decide what happens; the acts, the theme and the method decide what it means. Play every instruction as these two characters would live it, never as a label for the move.

### Act 1: the opening

Length: improvisations 1 to 3, about 150 words.

Both are presenting. The chat stays on the surface: the first boasts and jokes, what each one wants the other to think of them. Both are already toxic, but it only shows as tells they would not notice themselves. Plant here the detail that will pay off in act 3.

### Act 2: the match

Length: improvisations 4 to 8, about 250 words.

They get personal. To them it reads as intimacy and it should read as sincere, but each one's flaw lands exactly on the other's weak spot, so each step one takes rewards the other and both escalate. When a move misses, it is the same mechanism costing them this time, not a change of subject. The reader sees the mechanism; neither character ever names it. The reader also finds out why each of them does their act.

### Act 3: the closing

Length: improvisations 9 to 11, about 100 words.

The chat turns concrete: sex, a date, a time and a place, as specifically as the brief wants. The plant from act 1 pays off here, ideally inside the proposal itself. How it ends is not your choice: the last improvisations tell you. Either the two dysfunctions fit and they get what they want (positive does not mean healthy), or the mechanism from act 2 tips over and it crashes. Not every thread needs tying up.

### The theme for the whole chat

**Not recognised by your own people**

The character is sure he's forgettable, keeps reintroducing himself, and can't take it in when the other one remembers.

What it is: In Wright's work the self keeps slipping. The narrator can win a petty argument about his things and still go unrecognised by the person he lives with, and he tells it as if the furniture were the main event. Identity is something the world doesn't confirm.

In Pinder: The character believes he's forgettable, and he has evidence: exes who called him the wrong name, matches who sent him the same opener twice. He's afraid of being interchangeable, one more dick on a screen of dicks. On the app he repeats distinguishing details, checks that the other one remembers what he said, and introduces himself again mid-chat. The reader sees that the other one did remember, and that he can't take it in.

### The method for the whole chat

**The outrageous ask in the politest voice**

A request far over the line, wrapped in the tidiest, most considerate phrasing the character has.

How it works: The register and the content contradict each other: the manners of a polite note around an insane request. The politeness makes refusing socially awkward, so the reader watches the other person squirm inside their own good manners.

In Pinder: The character asks for something intimate, premature or logistically mad with a 'no rush' or an offer to go first. The filth goes in plain, specific detail; the courtesy goes in the frame around it. It works best once their act is established, so the politeness reads as how they cope rather than as a writer's trick.

Test: Strip the politeness: is the request outrageous? Read only the politeness: is it a perfectly normal message?

Goes wrong when: Sarcastic politeness is a wink. A generic ask ('wanna fuck') gives nothing away about the character.
