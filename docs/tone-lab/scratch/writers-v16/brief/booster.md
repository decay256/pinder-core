Here’s how I’m reading this.

### What we’re actually making

Pinder is a comedy game that looks and plays exactly like a dating app—profiles, a chat window, dice rolls, and a literal meter at the top of the screen tracking how much the other person is into you. The twist, if you can even call it a twist, is that every user on it is a sentient penis. Not a guy acting like a dick: an actual shaft and balls with an iPhone, a carefully curated bio, a little outfit or accessory it thinks draws the eye away from its flaws, and deep, unresolved trauma from puberty. Because everyone on the app is a dick, the things we usually spend hours agonizing over in secret—length, girth, veins, a weird curve, which nut hangs lower—aren't subtext here. That’s just how you say hello. It’s R-rated, it’s deeply filthy, and we shouldn't be precious about it.

The reason this works as satire is that it’s barely a metaphor. Anyone who has ever been on an app at one in the morning knows you’re already reducing yourself to a sentient genital under harsh bathroom lighting just to feel something. Dating apps take people who are lonely, horny, scared, and desperate to be wanted, and flatten them into three photos and a height requirement. They turn human connection into a video game with a scoreboard, and because the house only makes money if you stay lonely, the whole machine is rigged to keep you swiping. So you put on a pathetic little performance of who you think people want to fuck, you copy-paste the same opener to forty strangers because the algorithm rewards volume over soul, and the second things get slightly awkward, the next match is one thumb-twitch away. 

Pinder just makes the quiet part loud. The gamification is visible. The objectification is literal. You are a piece of meat trying to beat the house, and you’re probably going to lose.

So the tone is fast, absurd, and nasty about the *app*—and about the ridiculous armor people build to survive it. But the golden rule here is that we are never, ever cynical about the people or the fact that they want to be loved. If we’re just mocking them for having needs, it’s mean and it dies on the page. The filth and the farce only work if you fully believe these two talking dicks are real, bruised people who want something genuine and are doing a terrible job of hiding it.

### How a chat needs to work

A player is reading this whole thing in a chat window on their phone. In just that span of texts, they have to actually get to know two human beings.

First, you make the reader laugh at the delusion—the hyper-specific, weird little act each character is putting on to seem desirable or unbothered. Then, you let the mask slip just enough that we see *why* they’re doing that bit. The jokes don't stop, they just start stinging a little, because now you recognize the panic underneath. You like them, or you ache for them. And then you smash their two coping mechanisms into each other and see what happens.

**If they end up together:** It has to feel earned. We need to know what both of them were terrified of, and we need to watch the exact moment they each decide to drop the act for the other person. It should be funny and genuinely sweet, and the reader has to know why it’s *these two specific weirdos* who unlocked each other, not just any warm body.

**If they crash and burn:** The reader should laugh, wince, and think, *yep, that’s why we’re all single.* Nobody is the villain. In fact, it only really hurts if you can see that they actually would have been great together—right up until one person's defense mechanism slams directly into the other person's exact sore spot, and the app makes it way too easy to bail. 

Winning or losing the game mechanics isn't the point. If a player gets a date at the end and feels nothing, we failed. If they get unmatched and spend the next week thinking about it because it felt a little too much like their own text history, we nailed it.

### The litmus test

No matter how funny our jokes are, a chat doesn't work unless someone who *only* read those texts can answer four questions about **both** characters when it’s over:

1. What are they actually afraid of?
2. What is the obnoxious bit they do so they don't have to feel that fear?
3. Where did that damage come from?
4. What choice did they make at the very end?

If the reader can't clock all four for both dicks, the chat failed, and we rewrite it.
