## The Premise

Pinder is a dating app where everyone on it is a dick. Not metaphorically—though if you’ve ever been on Hinge at two in the morning, it’s a distinction without a difference. The joke, of course, is that we haven’t exaggerated anything. 

A real dating app takes a lonely, frightened human being who just wants to be held, reduces them to four photos and a credit score, and turns love into a fruit machine with an interest meter and a pair of dice. And like any casino, the house only makes money if you lose. If you find someone, you delete the app; if you stay miserable, you’re a subscriber. So the app trains you to be awful. It rewards the copy-pasted opener you’ve already sent to forty strangers, it crams your entire human worth into three inches of glass, and it keeps the next replacement one swipe away so nobody ever has to be brave.

Pinder just takes the metaphor and removes the trousers. The characters are reduced to their genitals because that’s what the algorithm does to you anyway. 

## The Target

In *The Naked Jape*, we talked about how every joke needs a target. Here is the one rule you cannot break: the target is the app, and the ridiculous armour people wear to survive it. The target is *never* the loneliness underneath.

It’s a farce, so we are completely cynical about the machine. We are never cynical about the people. If the reader doesn't believe these two ridiculous, dressed-up cocks are real people who desperately want to be loved and are catastrophic at asking for it, the comedy dies. We punch at the delusion, not the wound. Even I have standards, and I once hid three million quid in Jersey.

## The Mechanism

A good joke is a shift in perspective. A Pinder chat is the exact same trick, just stretched over five minutes on a phone screen.

1. **The Setup:** The reader meets two people and laughs at their act—the weird, specific, desperate little routine each one performs so they don't have to be vulnerable. 
2. **The Reveal:** The reader finds out *why* they do the routine. The laughing doesn't stop, it just gets a bruise on it. It turns affectionate, or it hurts.
3. **The Collision:** We watch the two acts run straight into each other.

**If they match:** It has to feel earned. We know what they’re terrified of, and we watch them both put the shield down anyway. It’s funny, it’s tender, and the reader knows why it had to be *these* two freaks and nobody else.

**If they crash:** Nobody is the villain. We can see both of them clearly, we can see they’d actually be good together, and then we watch one person’s defence mechanism land squarely on the other person’s trauma. The reader should laugh, wince, and think: *yes, that’s why dating is fucked.* It wasn't malice. It was the act, the fear, and the app.

Winning the game doesn't matter; winning the reader does. A date at the end that leaves the reader cold is a rewrite. An unmatch that makes them laugh and ache for a week is doing its job.

2. **What’s the act they put on so they don’t have to feel it?**

4. **What did they choose to do at the end—drop the act, or double down?**

If the reader can’t answer all four for both dicks, the chat goes in the bin. No matter how good the jokes were.
