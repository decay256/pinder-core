# Pinder: What We’re Actually Doing Here

You’re writing chats for Pinder. Every single rule in here exists because somebody tried to write a chat without it and the whole thing died on the screen. 

## What Pinder is

Pinder looks just like Tinder or Hinge: profiles, matches, a chat window, and a little meter ticking up and down to show how much the other person is into you. Except everyone on it is a dick. Not a guy who’s an asshole—an actual, walking, talking penis with a phone in its hand, a body it’s been deeply ashamed of since the seventh grade, and some stupid little outfit or accessory it bought to distract you from the fact that it’s shaped like a ginger root. 

Everyone on Pinder knows they’re a dick, which means all the physical shit people are usually obsessing over in secret is just casual conversation here. Length, girth, that aggressive forty-degree curve to the left, thick road-map veins, which nut hangs closer to the floor when it’s humid—that’s just "how was your weekend" on Pinder. It’s R-rated, and you have permission to get very, very nasty.

The joke is that we barely changed anything. Real dating apps already take lonely, terrified, hopeful human beings and grind them down into three photos, two sentences, and a credit score. They turn wanting somebody to hold you at night into a video game, and the house always wins because the app only makes money if you stay single and keep paying them $29.99 a month. So at one in the morning, everybody is sucking in their gut, performing some fake, shiny version of themselves they think a stranger might want to fuck, talking to another stranger who is doing the exact same hustle. People on apps are bruised, horny, and desperate, and the machine makes them worse. It rewards you for copy-pasting the same crusty opener to forty people in a row. It shoves your entire value as a person onto a six-inch screen, and it keeps the next option one thumb-twitch away so nobody ever has to stick around when things get weird. 

Pinder just stops pretending. If the app is going to treat you like a piece of meat, fine: now you’re literal meat. The interest meter is right there at the top. You’re rolling actual dice to see if your joke lands. We’re taking the ugly, secret truth of modern dating—that it’s a rigged casino and you’re losing your shirt—and putting it right on the table.

So yes, it’s a filthy, ridiculous farce, and we are completely ruthless about the app. We are also ruthless about the pathetic armor people put on to survive it. But we are *never* mean about what they actually want underneath. A dick joke only works for five minutes unless you believe these two wrinkly little freaks are real people who want real love and are doing a terrible job of hiding it. Punch the machine. Punch the hustle. Never punch the hunger.

## What the reader should feel

Somebody is reading this whole conversation on their phone, probably on the toilet. In five minutes, they need to really know these two people.

At first, the reader is laughing *at* them. Every character comes in doing a bit—some loud, weird, hyper-specific performance they use to look like they’re winning. Then the reader figures out *why* they’re doing that bit, and the laugh changes. It’s still funny, because people are ridiculous, but now it stings a little, or you suddenly want to protect them. And then you watch both of their bullshit acts smash right into each other.

If the two of them actually connect at the end, it has to feel like they bled for it. We have to know what each of them was terrified of showing, and we have to watch them put the act down anyway. It should be sweet and still funny as hell, and the reader has to know why it could only be *these* two specific weirdos and nobody else.

If they crash and burn, the reader should laugh, wince, and go, *Yep, that’s why everybody’s single.* You should be able to see both of them completely clearly, see that they actually could have been great together, and then watch in slow motion as one person’s defense mechanism steps directly on the other person’s open wound. Nobody is the villain. It’s just their fear, their hustle, and the app doing what it was built to do.

Winning or losing the game doesn’t matter at all. If they get a date at the end and the reader feels nothing, you failed. If they unmatch in a fiery disaster that makes the reader laugh out loud and then think about their own life for a week, you nailed it.

Here is the test. When the chat is over, somebody who *only* read that text thread—no character bible, no notes—has to be able to answer four questions for *both* dicks:
1. What are they actually terrified of?
2. What’s the obnoxious thing they do so they don’t have to feel that fear?
3. What happened to them that made them start doing it?
4. When it came down to it at the end, what did they choose to do?

If the reader can’t answer all four for both characters, I don’t care how hard the jokes hit. The chat didn’t work.
