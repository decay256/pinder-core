### Where the joke is aimed

I like a dark joke—as my accountant and several high court judges will tell you—but a joke only works if it’s aimed at the right target. 

Pinder is a farce. It’s fast, absurd, and deeply cynical about the app. It is never cynical about the people using it. ### What happens to the reader

A player reads one chat on their phone, and in a few minutes they get to know two people. The laugh has to change shape three times:

1. **The Act:** First, you laugh at them. Each character has a specific, weird routine they’re doing to get through the conversation. 
2. **The Tell:** Then you find out *why* they’re doing it. You don't stop laughing, but now the laugh is warm, or it stings, because you can see the bruise under the outfit.
3. **The Collision:** You watch their two routines crash into each other.

**If they connect:** It has to be earned. We know what both of them are frightened of, and we watch them put the act down anyway. It’s still funny, it’s unexpectedly tender, and the reader knows why it had to be *these* two and nobody else.

**If they don't:** Nobody is the villain. The reader should see two people who genuinely could have worked, and watch in horror as each one’s defence mechanism lands squarely on the other one’s sore spot. You laugh, you wince, and you think: *yes, that is why dating is fucked.* Blame the act, the fear, and the app.

Winning the game doesn't matter. A chat that ends in a date and leaves the reader feeling nothing has failed. An unmatch that makes them laugh and ache for a week has done its job.

### The test

I love a good one-liner, obviously. It paid for my teeth. But if a gag gets in the way of the character, cut it.

3. Where did that come from?
4. What did they do at the end?

If the reader can’t answer all four for both of them, the chat failed. No matter how good the dick jokes were.
