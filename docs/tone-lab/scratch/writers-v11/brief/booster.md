Here’s what we’re doing here, and why it actually matters.

## What Pinder is

We’re writing a game, and we’re writing satire. On the surface, Pinder looks like every dating app that has ever ruined your Tuesday night: profiles, matches, a chat window, plus a literal meter ticking up and down to show you how much the other person is losing interest in real time. 

The catch is that everyone on the app is a dick. Not "a guy who listens to podcasts," a literal, sentient penis. With a phone, a profile, a little outfit or accessory it desperately hopes draws the eye away from its flaws, and a body it has been apologizing for since puberty. Everyone on Pinder knows what they are, which means all the physical anxieties we usually pretend aren't running the show—length, girth, the curve, the vein situation, which ball hangs lower—aren't subtext here. That’s just small talk. It is R-rated, and you have full permission to be completely filthy.

The joke underneath all of it is that this is barely a stretch. 

If you’ve spent any time on the apps, you know what they do. They take lonely, terrified, hopeful human beings and flatten them into four photos and a prompt about pineapple on pizza. They turn the basic human desire to be loved into a video game with a scoreboard, and—here’s the darkest part—their entire business model relies on you losing, because if you actually meet someone, you delete the app. 

So at one in the morning, we all log on and perform whatever version of ourselves we think is most fuckable to a stranger who is doing the exact same thing. Everyone is horny, everyone is bruised, everyone is terrible at this, and the app is engineered to bring out our worst instincts. It rewards the copy-pasted opener you’ve already road-tested on forty other people. It reduces your entire value as a person to what fits inside a rectangle. And because the next match is literally one thumb-twitch away, nobody ever has to stick around when things get slightly weird. 

Pinder just takes the subtext and makes it the UI. The characters are reduced to their genitals because that’s how the apps treat us anyway. The interest meter is visible. The dice rolls are right there on screen. It’s dating stripped of the illusion that it isn't a game you’re probably losing.

So yes, it’s a farce. It should be fast, absurd, disgusting, and completely cynical about the machine. **But we are never cynical about the people.** The filth only works if the reader genuinely believes these two dicks are real people who want something real and are just doing a catastrophic job of hiding it. We are punching at the algorithm, and we are punching at the insane armor people build to survive it. We are never punching at the fact that they want to be wanted.

## How a chat has to work

Someone is reading this whole conversation on their phone. In five minutes, they need to actually know these two people.

First, you make the reader laugh at the bit. Every character comes into the chat doing an act—some hyper-specific, weird little routine they’ve developed to seem impressive or untouchable. Then, you let the mask slip just enough for the reader to see *why* they do it. The reader shouldn't stop laughing,</strong> but the laugh should change. It gets affectionate, or it stings a little, because now they recognize the bruise underneath. 

And then you smash those two acts into each other.

If they end up connecting, it has to feel like they earned it. We need to know what both of them were terrified of, and we need to watch them actively choose to put the shield down for each other. It stays funny, it gets tender, and it has to be completely clear why it worked with *these* two specific weirdos and wouldn't have worked with anyone else.

If they crash and burn, the reader should laugh, wince, and think: *yep, that is why dating is fucked.* We should be able to see both of them completely clearly, see that they actually could’ve been great together, and then watch in slow motion as Person A’s defense mechanism lands directly on Person B’s open wound. Nobody is the villain. It’s just the act, the fear, and the app doing what the app does.

Winning or losing the game does not matter. A chat that ends in a date but leaves the reader feeling nothing is a failure. An unmatch that makes them laugh and then haunts them for a week because it was too real? That’s a win.

## The test

When a chat is over, a reader who knows nothing else about these characters except the messages they just read has to be able to answer four questions about *both* of them:

1. What are they actually afraid of?
2. What’s the bit they do so they don’t have to feel it?
3. Where did that damage come from?
4. What choice did they make at the end?

If the reader can’t answer all four for both characters, I don't care how funny your jokes are—the chat didn't work.
