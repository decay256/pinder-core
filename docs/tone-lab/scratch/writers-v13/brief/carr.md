Here’s the brief, stripped down to the chassis.

### What Pinder is

Pinder looks exactly like a dating app—profiles, matches, a chat window, dice rolls, and a live meter tracking how interested they are—except everyone on it is a dick. Not metaphorically. A literal, sentient penis with a smartphone, an ill-advised outfit or accessory, and a body it’s been apologising for since puberty. Because everyone on the app is a set of genitals, length, girth, curve, vein-work, and which testicle hangs lower aren't dirty talk. They're small talk. It’s R-rated, and it is filthy. 

The joke is that we’ve barely exaggerated anything. 

Real dating apps take lonely, terrified, hopeful human beings and reduce them to three photos, a two-line bio, and an algorithm. They turn the basic human need for love into a game with a scoreboard, run by a casino that only makes money if you leave empty-handed and come back tomorrow. So at one in the morning, people who are hurt, horny, and desperate put on a front they think a stranger might want, paste an opener they’ve already used forty times, and know that the next option is one thumb-twitch away. Nobody has to stay.

Pinder just makes the subtext literal. The app treats you like a piece of meat, so we made you a piece of meat. The gamification is right there on the screen, and as in real life, you are probably losing.

### Where the joke is aimed

I like a dark joke—as my accountant and several high court judges will tell you—but a joke only works if it’s aimed at the right target. 

Pinder is a farce. It’s fast, absurd, and deeply cynical about the app. It is never cynical about the people using it. We make fun of the machine, and we make fun of the ridiculous armour people build to survive the machine. We never make fun of the fact that they want to be loved. If the reader doesn't believe these two talking penises are real people hiding real damage, the filth is just admin.

### What happens to the reader

A player reads one chat on their phone, and in a few minutes they get to know two people. The laugh has to change shape three times:

1. **The Act:** First, you laugh at them. Each character has a specific, weird routine they’re doing to get through the conversation. 
2. **The Tell:** Then you find out *why* they’re doing it. You don't stop laughing, but now the laugh is warm, or it stings, because you can see the bruise under the outfit.
3. **The Collision:** You watch their two routines crash into each other.

**If they connect:** It has to be earned. We know what both of them are frightened of, and we watch them put the act down anyway. It’s still funny, it’s unexpectedly tender, and the reader knows why it had to be *these* two and nobody else.

**If they don't:** Nobody is the villain. The reader should see two people who genuinely could have worked, and watch in horror as each one’s defence mechanism lands squarely on the other one’s sore spot. You laugh, you wince, and you think: *yes, that is why dating is fucked.* Blame the act, the fear, and the app.

Winning the game doesn't matter. A chat that ends in a date and leaves the reader feeling nothing has failed. An unmatch that makes them laugh and ache for a week has done its job.

### The test

I love a good one-liner, obviously. It paid for my teeth. But if a gag gets in the way of the character, cut it. 

When the chat is over, a reader who has seen *only* that chat has to be able to answer four questions about **both** characters:

1. What are they afraid of?
2. What is the act they put on so they don't have to feel it?
3. Where did that come from?
4. What did they do at the end?

If the reader can’t answer all four for both of them, the chat failed. No matter how good the dick jokes were.
