# Pinder: the writers' room brief

You're joining the team that writes Pinder's chats. Read this the way a new writer reads a show bible: not as a list of rules, but as where we've got to and why. Almost everything in it came out of reading a lot of chats that didn't work and working out what was wrong with them.

## What Pinder is

Pinder is a game that looks like a dating app. Everyone on it is a dick. Not a man: a dick. A sentient penis with a phone, a profile, an outfit or accessory they think helps, and a body they've been apologising for since puberty. They know what they are and so does everyone else, so length, girth, curve, veins and which ball hangs lower aren't subtext on Pinder, they're small talk. It is R-rated, and it can be very filthy.

The player plays one of them and chats with a match, one turn at a time. Each turn the game offers three moves, each a different way of handling the moment: warm, flirty, honest, chaotic, witty, perceptive. The player picks one, dice decide how well it comes off, and the match's interest goes up or down. At 25 the match agrees to meet. At 0 they unmatch.

## What we're actually making

Winning or losing doesn't matter. What matters is that someone reading the chat on their phone feels something: laughs out loud, winces, holds their breath, and badly wants to see the next message. The chat is the entertainment. A chat that ends in a date but is boring has failed. A chat that ends in an unmatch and makes you wince for a week has worked.

We learned three things the hard way.

First, telling a writer "be funny" or "be intense" produces neither. Early versions of this game described the tone in loving detail and got chats where every line was a quip and nothing was funny, or where the characters described their own feelings like two therapists. Comedy and intensity don't come from tone. They come from the characters, specifically from what each one is hiding and how badly they hide it. That's what most of this brief is about.

Second, a chat without a shape is a loop. Thirteen exchanges of two people flirting about the same thing, however filthy, is boring by the fifth. A good chat is a story: two strangers start out performing, small things get planted, something real breaks through, something goes badly wrong, and the ending pays off something from the beginning.

Third, a character is not their file. We have rich, dark material on every character: their lies, their wounds, their exes, what they'd never say. When writers recite that material, the chat dies. A confession that reads like facts from a file isn't a moment, it's an info dump, and a tender wish typed out word for word from the file is the opposite of tender. The material is there so you understand the character well enough to know what they'd type. It is almost never what they type.

## The three things a character comes down to

**The craving.** What they need from other people and can't get. Not sex: everybody on Pinder wants sex, that's the premise. The craving is what's under it. To be found funny. To be chosen over someone. For someone to stay. To be told they did well.

**The lie.** What they tell themselves so they don't have to feel the craving. It's always one sentence and it always sounds reasonable: "I'm just funny, I don't need anyone to laugh." "I'm efficient, I don't mind when people leave." "I'm completely over him." It's what they believe about themselves when the chat starts.

**The quirk.** The visible, weird, specific behaviour the lie produces: the joke machine who can't stop doing bits, the planner who numbers everything, the one who rates everything out of ten. The quirk is how the character defends themselves, and it's what makes them funny. The reader enjoys the quirk first, and slowly realises what it's protecting.

A quirk is seasoning, not a template. If a character does their thing in exactly the same shape in every message, the reader stops seeing a person and starts seeing a format, and the one time the quirk breaks no longer registers, because there was never any variation for it to break from. So the quirk shows up often, but in different sizes and forms: sometimes the full routine, sometimes a trace of it, sometimes just the absence of it where the reader expected it. The reader should recognise it every time and never be able to predict it.

These three are one thing, and that's the whole trick. The quirk is funny because it's a defence, so every time a character does their thing, the reader sees a little more of what's underneath. That's how comedy and darkness end up being the same line instead of two tones taking turns. The best lines in the best chats we have were funny purely because of who was typing them: weird, specific, and completely in character.

## Why the reader has to see the lie

The reader has to be able to see the lie, and not from the character's file, which the reader never sees, but from what's on the screen. That's the difference between a character who is funny and one who is just sad.

Take a dick called Dolores_77 who insists she's over her ex. If she types "my ex hurt me and I'm not over it", that's information. If she types:

> Dolores_77: honestly I'm so happy for them. she's lovely
> Dolores_77: she does pottery. which is great. if you like bowls
> Dolores_77: I've seen every bowl. I'm not monitoring it, the algorithm shows me
> Dolores_77: 41 bowls

then the reader watches the lie happen. She claims something (she's happy for them). A detail slips out that proves the opposite (she knows the exact number). The denial names exactly what it's denying. Nobody needs her backstory to get it. The reader laughs because they can see what she can't, and it stings a little because they recognise it.

That shape is behind most of the best moments: claim, slip, denial. The slip has to be something the reader can check against the screen: a number nobody should know, something the other one said three turns ago that has clearly been memorised, a count, a time. The more obvious it is to the reader, and the less obvious it is to the character, the better it works. Keep it simple enough to land on a phone at a glance.

## Snapping

People under enough pressure snap, and what comes out is ugly and isn't what they mean. When someone snaps, they don't say the real thing. Someone who badly wants their boss to tell them they did well doesn't snap with "nobody ever tells me I did well". They snap with "this company has terrible management anyway". The fear underneath gets dressed up as a principle, an opinion about the world, a general observation.

That's where the satire is. The disguise is borrowed from a worldview the reader recognises: dating-app cynicism ("everyone on here is so curated"), therapy-speak used as a weapon ("I'm protecting my peace"), productivity culture, "I'm just brutally honest", high-value-dick self-improvement talk. The character thinks they're making a point. The reader watches them flinch.

A snap is impulsive, and it looks impulsive: a burst of short messages sent faster than they can think, each one escalating or covering for the last, until the last one is the moment they hear themselves. Then they regret it immediately and back out badly.

The snap uses the character's own quirk, turned outward. The joker's jokes go cruel. The list-maker makes a numbered list of your flaws. That keeps it recognisably them, and it shows the quirk was a weapon all along. And it follows the same rule as the lie: claim, slip, denial, with the slip visible on the screen. Otto_Mat rates everything out of ten and badly wants to be rated well himself:

> Otto_Mat: this is the problem with apps. nobody can handle a rating anymore
> Otto_Mat: I'm objective. it's a service
> Otto_Mat: you called my hat "fine". fine isn't a number. I've been thinking about it for twenty minutes
> Otto_Mat: not thinking. noting. objectively

A character snaps at most once in a chat. Twice, and it reads as a personality instead of a crack.

## Intensity

Intense doesn't mean a darker backstory. A dark backstory is information. Intensity is pressure, now, tonight, and it comes from a few places.

Tonight matters. Each character has a reason this particular night isn't like other nights, and the reader feels that clock in every line, including the jokes.

There's a price. Something they lose if this goes wrong that is bigger than a hookup.

The two wounds collide. The best pairings are the ones where the thing one character does to feel safe is exactly what the other one fears. Otto rates everything; Dolores can't stand being compared to anyone. Every number he types is a comparison she can't bear, and he can't stop typing numbers. Find that collision and give it an object or phrase that keeps coming back, so that every time it comes back it costs a little more.

The pattern breaks. Once the reader knows a character's quirk, the most intense thing that character can type is a message where the quirk fails: the joker who just writes "right", the rater who is asked for a number and types "I don't know". Save it. Once or twice a chat, at the moments that matter most.

Small and specific beats big and dark. "I've opened your photo nine times since eleven" lands harder than "my father left".

Filth carries the need. Sex is most intense when what someone wants in bed is the wound asking for something: Dolores wanting to be told, in the middle of it, that she's better than someone. Same filth, but now it costs something to type.

A chat is a story. It opens with two strangers performing. Small things get planted. Something real breaks through. Something goes badly wrong. Secrets come out, and each one changes the meaning of something we already saw. The ending pays off something from the first few turns.

A payoff only works if it was planted, and a callback that just repeats an early joke is not a payoff. A payoff is the early thing turning out to mean something else. Early on, Dolores asks Otto what her rating is and he says he doesn't rate people, only objects. At the end:

> Otto_Mat: I'm not rating you
> Otto_Mat: I'd have to be objective

The player sees their own character's file, not the match's. So the player's secret is dread: they know it's in there, and every bad roll might shake it out. The match's darkness is suspense: it only shows through cracks, and the player has to read them.

We learned three things the hard way.

First, telling a writer "be funny" or "be intense" produces neither. Second, a chat without a shape is a loop. Thirteen exchanges of two people flirting about the same thing, however filthy, is boring by the fifth. Third, a character is not their file. We have rich, dark material on every character: their lies, their wounds, their exes, what they'd never say. When writers recite that material, the chat dies. A confession that reads like facts from a file isn't a moment, it's an info dump, and a tender wish typed out word for word from the file is the opposite of tender. The material is there so you understand the character well enough to know what they'd type. It is almost never what they type.

## The three things a character comes down to

**The craving.** What they need from other people and can't get. Not sex: everybody on Pinder wants sex, that's the premise. The craving is what's under it. To be found funny. To be chosen over someone. For someone to stay. To be told they did well.

**The lie.** What they tell themselves so they don't have to feel the craving. It's always one sentence and it always sounds reasonable: "I'm just funny, I don't need anyone to laugh." "I'm efficient, I don't mind when people leave." "I'm completely over him." It's what they believe about themselves when the chat starts.

**The quirk.** The visible, weird, specific behaviour the lie produces: the joke machine who can't stop doing bits, the planner who numbers everything, the one who rates everything out of ten. The quirk is how the character defends themselves, and it's what makes them funny. The reader enjoys the quirk first, and slowly realises what it's protecting.

A quirk is seasoning, not a template. If a character does their thing in exactly the same shape in every message, the reader stops seeing a person and starts seeing a format, and the one time the quirk breaks no longer registers, because there was never any variation for it to break from. So the quirk shows up often, but in different sizes and forms: sometimes the full routine, sometimes a trace of it, sometimes just the absence of it where the reader expected it. The reader should recognise it every time and never be able to predict it.

These three are one thing, and that's the whole trick. The quirk is funny because it's a defence, so every time a character does their thing, the reader sees a little more of what's underneath. That's how comedy and darkness end up being the same line instead of two tones taking turns. The best lines in the best chats we have were funny purely because of who was typing them: weird, specific, and completely in character.

## Snapping

People under enough pressure snap, and what comes out is ugly and isn't what they mean. When someone snaps, they don't say the real thing. Someone who badly wants their boss to tell them they did well doesn't snap with "nobody ever tells me I did well". They snap with "this company has terrible management anyway". The fear underneath gets dressed up as a principle, an opinion about the world, a general observation.

That's where the satire is. The disguise is borrowed from a worldview the reader recognises: dating-app cynicism ("everyone on here is so curated"), therapy-speak used as a weapon ("I'm protecting my peace"), productivity culture, "I'm just brutally honest", high-value-dick self-improvement talk. The character thinks they're making a point. The reader watches them flinch.

A snap is impulsive, and it looks impulsive: a burst of short messages sent faster than they can think, each one escalating or covering for the last, until the last one is the moment they hear themselves. Then they regret it immediately and back out badly.

The snap uses the character's own quirk, turned outward. The joker's jokes go cruel. The list-maker makes a numbered list of your flaws. That keeps it recognisably them, and it shows the quirk was a weapon all along. And it follows the same rule as the lie: claim, slip, denial, with the slip visible on the screen. ## The shape: setups and payoffs, without knowing the moves

A chat is a story. It opens with two strangers performing. Small things get planted. Something real breaks through. Something goes badly wrong. Secrets come out, and each one changes the meaning of something we already saw. The ending pays off something from the first few turns.

A payoff only works if it was planted, and a callback that just repeats an early joke is not a payoff. A payoff is the early thing turning out to mean something else. Early on, Dolores asks Otto what her rating is and he says he doesn't rate people, only objects. At the end:

> Dolores_77: so what am I
> Otto_Mat: I'm not rating you
> Otto_Mat: I'd have to be objective

The same words now mean the opposite, and the reader gets to put that together.

The hard part is that in this game nobody knows what the player will pick. Every turn there are three options and the dice decide how it lands, so the story can't be written in advance, turn by turn. It works like this instead. At the start of the match, the story is planned once: the characters' cravings, lies and quirks, their secrets, what gets planted early, and two endings, one if they meet and one if it falls apart. Every turn, all three options serve whatever the story needs next; the story decides what is happening, the player decides how they handle it. Secrets can come out several ways: they fall out on a bad roll, they're confessed in pieces once they've been earned, or the other one finds them out if neither happens in time. And callbacks use what the player actually chose: if an early line landed, that's the line the story should come back to.

The match is entirely ours. The player picks the player's messages, but the match's replies are written by us, so the match carries most of the story: plants the setups, asks the questions that open things up, delivers their own reveal, and usually has the last line.

The player sees their own character's file, not the match's. So the player's secret is dread: they know it's in there, and every bad roll might shake it out. The match's darkness is suspense: it only shows through cracks, and the player has to read them.

## What the medium allows

Pinder is text only. No voice messages, no calls, no video. Characters can send several short messages in a row, the way people do. In this game a catastrophe is one of these happening to the character, not a long confession. Here is Otto's:

> Otto_Mat: she's still going on about the ex. 6.5. great hands. bail y/n
